Sparse Low-Rank Compensation for FFN Activation Sparsity

Zeroing most of a feed-forward layer's intermediate neurons is nearly free at moderate sparsity and ruinous at high sparsity. This report develops a compensation term that restores what the dropped neurons contributed, and shows that approximating it as sparse plus low-rank — rather than low-rank alone — closes 79% of the quality gap at the same arithmetic cost on LLaMA-2-7B.

Notation

The layer we are modifying

Every method below changes one thing only: how a gated feed-forward layer combines its intermediate activations. Attention and all linear projections stay dense.

FFN(x) = W_d · i , i = u ⊙ g , u = W_u x , g = σ(W_g x) x ∈ ℝ^h hidden state entering the FFN (h = 4096) i ∈ ℝ^d intermediate activation, one per neuron (d = 11008) σ the model's own activation (SiLU here) ⊙ elementwise product

Neuron j contributes W_d[:,j] · i_j to the output. Activation sparsity means: compute only a subset of these d contributions per token.

Method 1 · baseline

TIS — Top-K Intermediate Sparsity

The standard baseline (the LaRoSA-style setup we reproduced earlier). For each token and each layer independently, score every neuron by the magnitude of its intermediate activation, keep the K largest, and drop the rest to zero.

score_j = |i_j| , m = top-K mask , K = ⌊(1 − s)·d⌋ y = W_d · (m ⊙ i) s target sparsity — the fraction of neurons zeroed m binary mask, recomputed per token, per layer

What it gets right. The selection is per-token and exact, so at moderate sparsity the kept neurons carry almost all of the signal: at s=0.5 perplexity rises only from 5.474 to 5.522.

Where it breaks. Dropping a neuron sets its contribution to zero, but the true value is not zero — it is merely small. Those errors accumulate across 11008 neurons and 32 layers. At s=0.9 perplexity jumps to 8.110, a degradation of +2.64. Everything that follows is an attempt to pay back that accumulated bias cheaply.

Method 2 · the framework

MGR — Mean-Gated Residual sparsity

Instead of replacing a dropped neuron with zero, replace it with its expected value. Split the gate into a calibration mean plus a per-token deviation:

g_j = ḡ_j + (g_j − ḡ_j) ḡ_j = E[g_j] over a calibration corpus i_j = u_j·ḡ_j + u_j·(g_j − ḡ_j) └── predictable ──┘ └──── residual r_j ────┘

The residual r_j = u_j(g_j − ḡ_j) is what genuinely varies per token, and it is measurably more concentrated than i_j — so we select on it. The predictable part is kept for every neuron, including dropped ones. Writing that out:

y = W_d(m ⊙ i) + W_d((1−m) ⊙ (ḡ ⊙ u)) = W_d(m ⊙ i) + M x − W_d(m ⊙ (ḡ ⊙ u)) where M = W_d · diag(ḡ) · W_u ∈ ℝ^(4096 × 4096)

This is the key structural fact of the whole report. The mean-gate correction for all dropped neurons collapses into a single fixed matrix–vector product Mx, independent of the mask, precomputable offline. The third term is a cheap subtraction over kept neurons only.

Result. Computing Mx exactly — call it MGR-Exact — cuts the baseline's degradation at s=0.9 from +2.64 to +1.16, an improvement of 8.110 → 6.638 perplexity. That is the ceiling every approximation below is chasing.

Why an approximation is needed at all

Mx costs h² = 16.8 M multiply–accumulates per token per layer, or 12.4% of the dense FFN. That is real money at inference time, so the deployable question is: how well can we approximate Mx for a fraction of that?

Method 3 · approximation A

MGR-LR — Low-Rank compensation

The textbook move: factor M by truncated SVD and keep the top r singular directions.

M ≈ B A , A ∈ ℝ^(r×h) , B ∈ ℝ^(h×r) rank-r SVD of M comp(x) = B(A x) cost 2hr MACs/token

Cost scales linearly in r: r=1024 costs 6.2% of the dense FFN, half of exact. The factors are shared by every token — one fixed subspace for the whole corpus.

Where it struggles. M is not strongly low-rank in the middle of the network. It takes rank ≈ 1270 on average (up to 1500 in layers 4–17) to capture 90% of its Frobenius energy, so a rank-512 truncation discards almost half the matrix. The error is the same for every token:

error(x) = (BA − M)x = −R x = −Σc=1..h R[:,c]·x_c R = M − BA, the discarded residual — every channel contributes

Empirically r=512 is worse than doing nothing (8.764 vs the baseline's 8.110 at s=0.9) — the approximation error exceeds the signal it was meant to restore. r=1024 finally helps (7.229), and r=2048 nearly reaches exact (6.710) but by then costs exactly as much as Mx itself.

Method 4 · approximation B

MGR-SLR — Sparse Low-Rank compensation

Keep the low-rank term, then spend the remaining budget differently: instead of buying more singular directions, apply the exact residual R to the input channels that matter most for this particular token. This grafts the R-Sparse (ICLR 2025) sparse/low-rank split onto the compensation branch.

R = M − BA residual, computed offline S(x) = indices of the k largest |x_c| chosen per token comp(x) = B(A x) + R · (m_x ⊙ x) cost 2hr + kh MACs/token

The consequence for the error is exact and easy to state: selected channels are perfectly compensated, and only the unselected ones contribute error.

MGR-LR error = −Σall c R[:,c]·x_c MGR-SLR error = −Σc ∉ S(x) R[:,c]·x_c largest-|x| terms removed

Comparing budgets fairly

The two knobs cost different amounts, so we convert both to an equivalent-rank budget B_eff: one unit of rank costs 2h MACs, one kept channel costs h, hence

B_eff = r + k/2 e.g. (r=256, k=1536) ≡ (r=1024) ≡ 6.2% of dense FFN

Every configuration compared below sits at exactly one of two budgets, B_eff = 1024 (+6.2% compute) or B_eff = 2048 (+12.4%), so differences in perplexity are differences in how the budget was spent, never how much.

Why a mixture wins — the mechanism

The two terms fail in complementary ways. Pure low-rank spreads a thin error across all 4096 channels. Pure sparse handles its k chosen channels perfectly but leaves the remaining channels facing the full, undamped matrix — and those are exactly the small-magnitude channels that an earlier whitening study found to carry loss-relevant signal. A mixed split lets the low-rank term damp everything globally first, then spends the rest zeroing out the largest per-token spikes.

Setup

How the experiments were run

The sparsity grid: s = 0.5, 0.7, 0.9

s is the fraction of intermediate neurons zeroed per token, per layer, held exactly by a top-K rule with K = ⌊(1−s)·d⌋. Measured sparsity came out at 0.500 / 0.700 / 0.900 in every run, so all methods are compared at identical per-token compute in the FFN core; the only difference between them is the compensation branch. The three levels probe three regimes:

  • s = 0.5 — the easy regime. The baseline is already within 0.05 perplexity of dense, leaving almost nothing for compensation to win.
  • s = 0.7 — the transition. Degradation becomes visible (+0.25) but still moderate; plain TIS still edges out every approximated compensation here.
  • s = 0.9 — the hard regime, and the decision point. The baseline degrades by +2.64 and the methods separate sharply.

Protocol

  • Model & data. LLaMA-2-7B in bfloat16; WikiText-2 test perplexity at sequence length 2048, using the identical evaluation pipeline as our earlier reproduction work so numbers are directly comparable.
  • Calibration. ḡ and the factors are estimated once, offline, from a C4 calibration set. No retraining, no fine-tuning of any weight.
  • Oracle masks. Masks are derived from the true activations of the current token (compute-then-mask). No predictor and no fused kernel is involved — this isolates the algorithmic question from the systems question, and applies identically to all four methods.
  • Noise floor. Re-running the same configuration across GPUs and attention backends moves perplexity by ≈0.001, so differences above ≈0.01 are signal.
Results

What the measurements show

Two readings stand out. First, approximated compensation only earns its keep at high sparsity: at s=0.5 and s=0.7 the cheap approximations are worse than plain TIS (MGR-SLR 5.596 vs TIS 5.522 at s=0.5; 5.753 vs 5.728 at s=0.7) — the error they introduce exceeds the bias they remove, and the crossover point lies somewhere between s=0.7 and s=0.9, not yet located. Only the exact form (MGR-Exact) beats TIS at every level. Second, at s=0.9 MGR-SLR sits closer to the exact ceiling than MGR-LR does, at identical cost.

Three consequences worth stating plainly

  • Rank 2048 is the point of no return. Since 2hr = h² when r = h/2 = 2048, any approximation at that budget costs exactly what the exact product costs. This is why the +12.4% row is a saturation check rather than a deployment candidate — the useful operating regime is B_eff ≤ 1024.
  • MGR-SLR trades memory capacity for arithmetic. It must keep the full residual R resident (h² per layer) even though it only reads k of its columns per token. Arithmetic and read bandwidth fall; stored parameters do not. In the r=0 case the stored matrix is literally M itself.
  • The mask cost is not counted, equally for everyone. Under oracle masking both the baseline's score and MGR's score need u and g, so the selection cost is identical across methods and cancels in every comparison here. A deployable system would replace it with a predictor — that is a separate piece of work.
Negative results

What we ruled out along the way

Five alternatives were tested and rejected. They are listed because each one narrows the design space for future work.

ApproachIdeaOutcome @ s = 0.9
Weight-aware scoringScore neurons by |i_j|·‖W_d[:,j]‖ instead of |i_j|8.276 — worse than plain scoring (8.110)
Second-moment gate meanUse ḡ*=E[u²g]/E[u²] in place of ḡ6.889 vs 6.638 — consistently worse

Hot-neuron sparse splitCompute the most important rank-1 neuron terms of M exactlyPremise inverted: removing them raises the rank needed for 90% energy from 1270 to 1470 — the "hot" terms are the low-rank structure

The recurring lesson across the last three rows: input-space reconstruction error does not predict perplexity across method families. Whitening reduced its own objective by 13% and still lost; an offline screen preferred the pure-sparse split that perplexity ranked second. Offline metrics are useful for pruning candidates and useless for picking the winner.

Limits

What this does not yet establish

  • One model, one metric. LLaMA-2-7B and WikiText-2 perplexity only. No downstream task accuracy, and no evidence yet that the ranking transfers to other model families.
  • Oracle masking. Masks come from true activations. A deployable system needs a predictor, and the quality of that predictor could reorder these results.
  • No kernel. The per-token channel selection and gather are simulated, not implemented. Their real cost is not in any number here.
  • The memory trade is unresolved. MGR-SLR reduces arithmetic while keeping a full h² matrix resident, which is the wrong direction for memory-capacity-bound deployments.
  • Narrow operating range at this budget. The approximated forms beat plain TIS only at s=0.9 on the measured grid; at s=0.5 and s=0.7 TIS is ahead. The crossover has not been located, and no measurement yet compares MGR-SLR against simply running TIS at a lower sparsity for the same total compute.

Immediate next steps

  • Probe below the current budget — B_eff of 512 and 256 — to find where sparse low-rank breaks down. The +12.4% results show the upper end is saturated, so the open question lives at the cheap end.
  • Allocate (r, k) per layer rather than uniformly, validated on perplexity rather than on an offline proxy.
  • Confirm on a zero-shot task suite before making any accuracy claim.
